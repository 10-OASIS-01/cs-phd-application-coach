## Start from evidence and prompts

Before drafting, collect:

- the exact prompt, word or page limit, formatting rule, and official URL;
- the program's admission model;
- an evidence inventory of projects and contributions;
- current faculty and program-fit notes;
- any policy on AI assistance, collaboration, or editing.

Create a prompt checklist and map every requested element to a paragraph or section. Do not reuse a document merely because two schools call it an “SOP.”

## Research narrative

Build a reusable narrative before school-specific prose. For each important project, answer:

1. What question or limitation motivated the work?
2. What context does a reader need?
3. What did the applicant personally decide, build, prove, analyze, or write?
4. Why were those choices reasonable?
5. What happened, including failures or ambiguous results?
6. What did the applicant learn about research and the underlying question?
7. What new question follows?

Use this to identify a thread across projects. Do not force unrelated experiences into a false linear destiny.

## Statement of purpose

Treat the SOP as an evidence-based argument about qualifications, focus, and fit—not an autobiography or a list of adjectives.

A flexible paragraph map:

1. **Research direction:** name the family of questions and why it matters. Establish technical substance without grand claims.
2. **Primary research experience:** explain the question, contribution, one important decision, evidence or result, and learning.
3. **Additional development:** show a complementary project, growth, collaboration, or methodological range.
4. **Synthesis and future questions:** connect prior work to questions the applicant is prepared to investigate next.
5. **Program-specific fit:** explain why particular people, research communities, and resources enable that work; include multiple plausible connections where appropriate.
6. **Closing:** state the preparation and goal without repeating the introduction or promising guaranteed impact.

This is not a mandatory six-paragraph formula. Merge or reorder sections to answer the actual prompt and fit the limit.

Prefer concrete claims:

- weak: “I am passionate, resilient, and innovative.”
- stronger: “When the model failed on compositional instructions, I separated perception errors from planning errors, built a controlled evaluation set, and found that the apparent planning gain disappeared when object aliases were held out.”

Tailor fit with current evidence. Name a faculty member only when the applicant can explain a real connection and a question—not as a ceremonial list.

Do not:

- open with a generic childhood story unless it materially explains the research trajectory;
- summarize the CV chronologically without interpretation;
- claim sole ownership of a team result;
- spend scarce space praising the university;
- present one technique as an irrevocable five-year plan;
- copy language from sample statements.

## Research statement or proposal

First determine the genre.

### Retrospective research statement

Emphasize the arc, contributions, decisions, and future program of work. Organize by questions or themes rather than a paper list.

### Prospective proposal

Include, when the prompt requests them:

- problem and motivating gap;
- focused research questions or hypotheses;
- relationship to prior work;
- proposed methods, data, experiments, or theory;
- evaluation and alternative explanations;
- feasibility, resources, ethics, and risks;
- expected contribution and a realistic work plan.

Show enough specificity to demonstrate research judgment while acknowledging that doctoral research changes. For an advertised project, connect to its actual objectives and selection criteria. For supervisor-developed proposals, preserve the applicant's intellectual contribution and confirm what the supervisor expects.

## Personal or history statement

Answer the exact prompt. Relevant material may include educational context, community work, access barriers, teaching, service, leadership, identity, or how experience shapes research questions.

The applicant does not owe a trauma narrative. Never intensify hardship for emotional effect. Include sensitive context only when the applicant chooses and it advances the requested explanation.

A useful structure:

1. relevant context or perspective;
2. specific action, contribution, or learning;
3. how it shapes future participation, research, mentoring, or community;
4. connection to the program prompt.

Avoid generic diversity language unconnected to action or thought.

## CV and optional website

Prioritize legibility and evidence over decoration. Common sections:

- education;
- research interests as a short, durable line if useful;
- research experience with role and contributions;
- publications and preprints with accurate status;
- selected projects, software, datasets, or hardware;
- talks, posters, teaching, service, awards, and relevant employment;
- technical methods and tools when they add information.

Use action plus contribution plus context. Distinguish “submitted,” “under review,” “preprint,” and “published.” Do not inflate authorship or venue status.

An optional website can make papers, code, posters, demos, and contact information easy to inspect. It cannot replace a weak application or inaccessible artifacts. Check mobile layout, alt text, links, PDF text extraction, and a clear contact path.

## Recommendation letters

Ask directly: “Would you be able to write a strong and specific letter for my PhD applications?” Give the person an easy way to decline.

Choose writers who can provide firsthand evidence of research potential and calibrate it—not simply the most famous names. A strong letter often explains:

- the writer's relationship to the applicant and duration of observation;
- the problem and the applicant's exact contribution;
- specific research judgment, independence, creativity, iteration, or collaboration;
- how the applicant responded to feedback or failure;
- a meaningful comparison group and the basis for comparison;
- readiness for doctoral research.

Prepare a packet containing:

- CV and current statement draft;
- research narrative or one-page project summary;
- program and deadline table with time zones and submission mechanism;
- selected work or links;
- short reminders of interactions and contributions the writer observed;
- any program-specific questions.

Send reminders at agreed intervals. Track submission status without implying that a portal's automated email is sufficient communication.

If a writer cannot provide a strong letter, help the applicant choose another writer or improve the packet. Never draft an undisclosed letter in the recommender's voice. If a recommender explicitly asks the applicant for a draft, discuss ethical and institutional expectations and focus on a factual evidence memo where possible.

## Faculty outreach

Follow the faculty member's and department's instructions first. Outreach is appropriate when there is a specific research connection or a useful procedural question.

Keep an email concise:

1. informative subject;
2. one-sentence identity and current context;
3. one concrete connection between the applicant's work or question and the faculty member's recent work;
4. one answerable question, often about active direction or advising plans;
5. link to a compact CV or homepage when useful;
6. courteous close.

Do not mass-mail name-swapped templates, attach an unsolicited long proposal, demand a meeting, or treat silence as rejection. Academic networks are unequal; thoughtful outreach can help outsiders become legible, but it is not a magic admissions trick.

## Tests and transcripts

Treat every rule as cycle-specific:

- GRE required, optional, not accepted, or not considered;
- English tests, section minimums, validity windows, and degree-based waivers;
- unofficial versus official transcripts at application time;
- translation, credential evaluation, grading scale, and score-delivery timing;
- document naming and portal upload limits.

Record the official page and `last_checked` date. If department and graduate-school pages conflict, ask admissions and retain the response.

## Responsible AI assistance

Allowed use depends on the program. Verify current policy before drafting.

Generally appropriate when permitted:

- organizing evidence and prompts;
- comparing versions;
- locating unclear reasoning or unsupported claims;
- reducing length while preserving meaning;
- checking whether each prompt element is answered;
- simulating reader questions or interview follow-ups.

Never use AI to invent facts, citations, research experience, faculty fit, hardship, identity, or results. Do not let an assistant replace the applicant's voice with generic polished prose. The applicant must verify every claim and understand every technical statement.

## Review rubrics

### SOP or research statement

- Does the opening name a substantive question rather than a slogan?
- Can a reader identify the applicant's contribution to each project?
- Are research decisions and learning visible?
- Does the future direction follow from the evidence without overpromising?
- Is program fit specific, current, and multidimensional?
- Does every paragraph earn space under the actual prompt?
- Are all facts, affiliations, paper titles, and faculty names verified?

### CV

- Is the research contribution legible in a 30-second scan?
- Are status labels and dates accurate?
- Are project roles distinguishable from team outcomes?
- Are the most informative artifacts linked and accessible?
- Is formatting readable without depending on color?

### Outreach email

- Is the reason for contacting this person specific?
- Does the message demonstrate understanding without flattery?
- Is the question answerable and not already resolved on an official page?
- Can it be read on a phone without scrolling through a biography?

### Final integrity check

- Applicant confirms every fact and sentence.
- No copied sample language or fabricated evidence remains.
- Program name, degree, faculty, and prompt all match the destination.
- AI use complies with the current policy.
