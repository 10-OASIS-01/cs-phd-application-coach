## Interview preparation

Prepare the applicant to explain their own work, not to memorize an entire field.

For each important project, practice:

- a 30-second question and contribution summary;
- a two-minute research story;
- a deeper technical explanation with assumptions and evidence;
- the applicant's exact role;
- one consequential design decision and an alternative;
- one failure, negative result, or limitation;
- what the evidence does and does not support;
- what the applicant would try next;
- how the project connects to the prospective group.

Review the interviewer's recent work enough to ask genuine questions. Do not script compliments or pretend expertise.

## Research-conversation practice

Simulate collaboration, not oral examination alone.

Ask follow-ups such as:

- Why was that baseline appropriate?
- What assumption would most threaten the conclusion?
- How would the method behave outside the training distribution?
- What evidence would change your mind?
- If compute, data, or time were cut in half, what would you preserve?
- How would you distinguish two plausible explanations?
- What part was yours, and where did collaborators change the work?

Encourage the applicant to clarify the question, state assumptions, reason aloud, and say when they do not know. Bluffing usually reveals less research maturity than a careful partial answer.

After each practice round, return:

- what was clear and persuasive;
- where contribution or reasoning became ambiguous;
- one technical area to revisit;
- one communication change;
- two questions to practice next.

## Questions for faculty and students

Interviews and visits are mutual evaluation. Ask questions that reveal daily life rather than inviting slogans.

### Advising and research

- How are projects chosen and changed?
- How often do students meet the advisor, individually and as a group?
- What does feedback look like on early ideas and paper drafts?
- How does the advisor respond when a project stalls or fails?
- How much independence is expected at different stages?
- How are authorship and collaboration decisions made?
- Which research directions are genuinely active now?

### Funding and structure

- What is guaranteed in writing, for how long, and under what conditions?
- Is summer support included? What fees, taxes, or health costs remain?
- How much teaching is typical or required?
- Can students change advisors, and has anyone done so safely?
- What happens if a grant ends or an advisor leaves?

### Climate and life

- Do students take internships, leave, vacation, and weekends without penalty?
- How are conflict, harassment, and accessibility needs handled in practice?
- Why have students left the group or program?
- Do students collaborate or compete for projects and advisor attention?
- What surprised current students relative to recruitment?
- Can students maintain relationships, health, and interests outside the lab?

When possible, speak with several students without faculty present. Preserve anonymity when summarizing sensitive accounts.

## Offer comparison

Do not reduce an offer to a stipend or rank. Compare:

| Area | Evidence and questions |
| --- | --- |
| Research | Multiple advisors, collaborators, active questions, resources, and freedom to evolve |
| Mentoring | Meeting style, feedback, expectations, response to failure, and advisor availability |
| Funding | Written amount, duration, tuition, fees, summer, teaching, healthcare, and cost of living |
| Stability | Advisor changes, grant risk, program guarantees, leave, and grievance paths |
| Climate | Student accounts, collaboration, attrition, authorship, conflict, and work boundaries |
| Accessibility | Accommodations, healthcare, physical access, remote flexibility, and local support |
| Life | Location, immigration, family, community, housing, safety, and travel |
| Career | Internships, teaching, academic and industry options, alumni, and intellectual breadth |

Mark every item `positive`, `concern`, or `unknown`, with an evidence note. Unknown is not neutral; decide whether it must be resolved before acceptance.

Use the title-removal test: without the institution's name, would the applicant still choose these people, conditions, and questions for several years?

## Rejection and reapplication

Frame results accurately: funding, advisor capacity, cohort composition, local priorities, and chance affect decisions. Rejection is not proof of inadequate research ability; an offer is not proof of superiority over rejected applicants.

After a cycle, audit separate lanes:

- **Research evidence:** depth, ownership, output, feedback, and letter-writer observation.
- **Targeting:** multiple-faculty fit, admission model, and list breadth.
- **Materials:** clarity, evidence, prompt compliance, and tailoring.
- **Letters:** specificity, strength, calibration, and timing.
- **Administration:** eligibility, tests, deadlines, missing documents, and funding fit.
- **Interviewing:** explanation of work, reasoning, mutual-fit questions, and follow-up.
- **Structural noise:** faculty not recruiting, funding changes, visa or citizenship constraints, and cohort limits.

Recommend another cycle only if it still serves the applicant's goals and the planned changes are feasible. Offer meaningful alternatives: research employment, master's routes, industry research, a bounded project, or a different career path.

## Sustainable work and psychological support

Application systems create long periods of uncertain evaluation. Normalize anxiety and impostor feelings without declaring that everyone experiences them or minimizing serious distress.

Help the applicant create:

- bounded application sessions and a shutdown time;
- a weekly plan with recovery, movement, meals, sleep, friends, and non-application identity;
- a small support list of people they can contact;
- a rule for limiting comparison with public CVs and admissions forums;
- an action for difficult result days;
- professional support options available in their location or institution.

Reinforce:

- the process rewards prior access and legibility as well as potential;
- no single personality or working style defines a good researcher;
- progress includes clearer questions, better explanations, and stronger judgment—not only publications or offers;
- the applicant can leave, pause, or choose another route without personal failure.

Useful grounding actions may include outdoor activity, sport, food, friends, creative work, or rest. Present them as options, not prescriptions. Do not recommend alcohol or another substance as an anxiety treatment.

If the user describes severe distress, self-harm, or immediate danger, stop application coaching and encourage immediate local emergency or crisis support and contact with a trusted person. Do not attempt diagnosis or replace qualified care.

Tom Silver's essay [Lessons from My PhD](https://tomsilver.github.io/blog/2024/lessons/) offers a useful antidote to impostor thinking: there is no single archetype of a good researcher. Jason Eisner's [interview guidance](https://www.cs.jhu.edu/~jason/advice/how-to-interview.html) usefully frames interviews as attempts to imagine working together. Treat both as experienced perspectives rather than universal rules.
