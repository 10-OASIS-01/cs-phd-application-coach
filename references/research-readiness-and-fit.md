## Decide whether a PhD fits

Start from the work the applicant wants to do, not the title they want to hold.

Ask:

- Does the goal require independent research training, or would a research job, master's, residency, or targeted project be a better experiment?
- Does the applicant enjoy prolonged ambiguity, failed approaches, reading, writing, and revising explanations—not only implementing known solutions?
- What opportunity cost, financial constraint, location constraint, or caregiving responsibility matters?
- Has the applicant observed actual research work closely enough to distinguish it from coursework or product engineering?

Do not use a single bad project or advisor as proof that research is a poor fit. When possible, recommend a bounded research experience before a multi-year commitment.

## Build an evidence inventory

Research potential is not a publication count. Inventory evidence under these headings:

- **Question formation:** noticed an ambiguity, failure mode, missing assumption, or important problem.
- **Research judgment:** chose baselines, data, metrics, theory, experiments, or scope and can explain why.
- **Technical contribution:** code, proof, system, dataset, analysis, annotation, hardware, or method.
- **Iteration:** diagnosed failure, changed course, or learned from negative results.
- **Communication:** paper, report, thesis, poster, talk, documentation, or clear oral explanation.
- **Collaboration:** incorporated feedback, coordinated work, supported others, or handled disagreement.
- **Persistence with boundaries:** sustained work without treating unhealthy overwork as virtue.
- **External evidence:** a recommender directly observed the above.

For each item, capture context, the applicant's exact role, one decision, one result, and what changed in their thinking. Use this inventory for the CV, statements, recommendation packet, and interviews.

## Obtain meaningful research exposure

Prioritize access to a real question and feedback over prestige. Possible routes include:

- a faculty or lab project at the home institution;
- a research internship, fellowship, or visiting program;
- a thesis, independent study, or course project extended into genuine inquiry;
- a reproducibility study, benchmark audit, dataset analysis, or open-source research contribution;
- research-adjacent work where the applicant can identify and investigate an unanswered question.

Avoid presenting a solo project as equivalent to mentored research when it lacked feedback or novelty evaluation. Instead, describe honestly what it demonstrates and seek critique from qualified researchers.

## Define a durable research direction

Build a coherent story around a family of questions:

1. What phenomenon, capability, or failure matters?
2. Why is it scientifically or socially important?
3. What has the applicant learned from prior work?
4. Which approaches appear promising, and what are their limits?
5. What questions would the applicant investigate next?

Prefer “I care about how embodied agents achieve compositional generalization and long-horizon decision-making; structured planning and learned world models are two routes I want to test” over a permanent identity tied to one current model family.

Coherence makes the application legible. It is not a promise to spend five years on one method.

## Discover programs and faculty

Use a question-to-people workflow:

1. Write three to five research questions or tensions.
2. Find recent papers that address them from different methods.
3. Trace authors, labs, collaborators, workshops, datasets, and citations.
4. Read current faculty and lab pages; confirm institutional affiliation and advising eligibility.
5. Inspect two or three recent works, not only a famous older paper.
6. Record both the overlap and a substantive question or difference.
7. Identify multiple plausible advisors, collaborators, or rotation options.

Search neighboring departments and institutes where appropriate, but verify that those faculty can formally advise or co-advise the target degree.

## Evaluate research and mentoring fit

Separate dimensions that applicants often collapse:

| Dimension | Evidence to seek |
| --- | --- |
| Question fit | Recent papers, talks, grants, active projects, and what the faculty member says now |
| Method fit | Theory, systems, data, hardware, human studies, clinical work, or mixed methods |
| Advising availability | Official recruiting note, direct answer, current group size, funding—not rumor |
| Mentoring style | Meeting frequency, feedback speed, direction versus independence, response to failure |
| Lab reality | Student experiences, collaboration, authorship, conflict handling, departures, workload |
| Resources | Compute, robots, data, clinical partners, participant access, fabrication, or domain expertise |
| Accessibility | Accommodations, healthcare, physical and digital access, leave, location, and culture |
| Career freedom | Internships, teaching, industry collaboration, academic placement, and non-academic paths |

Speak with several students when evaluating an offer. One enthusiastic or unhappy account is evidence, not the entire distribution.

Ask the title-removal question: if the university name and ranking disappeared, would the applicant still want to solve hard problems with these people for several years?

## Build a balanced list

Do not label PhD programs as deterministic “safety,” “target,” and “reach” categories. Instead, balance uncertainty:

- several programs with strong multi-faculty fit;
- more than one admission model or funding route where practical;
- realistic geographic, financial, accessibility, and family constraints;
- enough applications to absorb noise without making quality or cost unmanageable;
- a non-PhD or later-cycle alternative the applicant would genuinely accept.

For each program, write a one-sentence fit thesis and one-sentence risk. Remove programs that are famous but lack credible fit, funding, or a livable environment.

## Common reasoning failures

- **Prestige substitution:** using ranking as a proxy for advisor, resources, or life quality.
- **Single-advisor dependency:** applying where one unavailable faculty member collapses the case.
- **Paper-title matching:** claiming fit from keywords without understanding the work.
- **Technique lock-in:** defining identity by one fashionable method rather than a durable question.
- **Publication gatekeeping:** treating applicants without papers as incapable despite other evidence.
- **Outcome ranking:** interpreting offers and rejections as a global ordering of talent.
- **Public-page idealization:** assuming a lab's polished description is daily reality.
- **Self-rejection:** inferring ineligibility or poor fit without checking the actual rule or asking a specific question.
